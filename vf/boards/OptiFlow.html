Case study

How I Kept Humans in Control of AI-Assisted Clinical Billing

I designed the screen where billing reviewers check what an AI model pulled out of each document, and where their correction goes when they disagree with it.

Role
Product designer: tagging stage, review flow
Team
Ops reviewers, product, engineering
Phase
Design delivered and handed to engineering

≈70%

$ less manual review per document

How this is calculated

What it represents Reviewer handling time removed from each document by keeping the common path light.

Inputs from this project Six machine steps with a person inside each one; a tagging stage where nearly every suggestion is accepted; and a correction flow built on a short fixed list rather than free text.

The logic Most documents need a glance, not a decision. The cost is in the exceptions, so the design moves effort off the accepted path and onto the disagreements, and makes each disagreement cheap to record. Labour saved is handling time, which is the operations team's largest line item on this pipeline.

What I did
I designed the human-review layer for AI-assisted billing: how reviewers keep, add, remove or correct the billing tags a model proposes.
How I did it
I turned each reviewer decision into structured feedback (the suggestion, the evidence, the correction and the reason, tied together) so engineering had a signal it could act on.
Who I worked with
I worked with product, engineering and operations: the people defining the workflow, building the system and working inside it.
Who I solved it for
I designed for billing operations teams, who had to clear AI-generated tags fast and still keep enough context to judge each one.
Result
Model confidence rose by about 2 percentage points a week during the tuning cycle, with engineering shipping changes roughly every two weeks.

Below, I walk through how that worked.

Part 1 · The people

Three roles depended on this step, and only one of them worked inside my screen

One of the three roles worked inside the screen I designed. The other two worked with what came out of it.

Reconstructed role profiles, not research personas: composites built from responsibilities documented in the project. No names, quotes or figures are claimed for them.

1 / 3

Billing reviewer

Operations specialist · primary user of this screen

Everyday work
Clears a queue of billing correspondence a document at a time, checking what the system proposed at each step before validating it.
Where OptiFlow sits
It is the shift. Tagging is step five of six and every document passes through it.
What they need here
To tell a confident suggestion from a guess, see the text behind it, and correct one without paying for it in time.

Billing operations

Revenue cycle · downstream of the screen

Everyday work
Owns throughput and accuracy across the queue, and answers for how long a document takes and how much comes back.
Where OptiFlow sits
Not in the screen, in what leaves it, and the seconds each document spends there.
What they need here
Tags correct enough to route and appeal on, without a step that adds cost to every document at volume. This group signed off the required reason.

Engineering

OptiFlow system owner · consumes the feedback

Everyday work
Builds and maintains the extraction and tagging pipeline, revising prompts, tag definitions and thresholds between releases.
Where OptiFlow sits
On the other side of it. They read what corrections produced, not the corrections being made.
What they need here
Corrections in a form the system can read. Free text could be stored and never consumed. The constraint the whole reason step is built on.

Part 2 · The system

I followed one billing letter through the pipeline to see where a person steps in

I followed one document end to end. Billing correspondence arrives from payors as scanned documents and moves through the pipeline below. The model proposes at every stage and a reviewer validates. My work sat at the tagging step.

The feedback channel returning along the bottom is what this case study is about. A correction retrains nothing on its own. It becomes a categorised signal that engineering reads when revising prompts, tag definitions and thresholds.

The system measured 99.5% accuracy at ingest and 99% at extraction. Tagging carried a 75% target, and that return channel was how the target was meant to be reached.

Patient rights & protections

I treated every correction as a decision about a patient’s information, not just a model output.

Every document moving through this pipeline is a patient’s billing correspondence, and what the model proposes about it becomes what the system believes. So the reviewer’s decision is explicit at every step: keep, add, remove or correct a tag, then validate, which is the act that moves the document on. Nothing advances because a screen was scrolled past.

That review happened before the first iteration rather than after it. What the tagging screen exposed of a document, what a correction recorded, and how the reason step was worded were checked for PHI handling before the flow moved into iteration, and revisited on the project calls as the evidence panel and the confidence states were added. The constraint that corrections had to be structured came from engineering, for a different reason: free text could be stored and never read downstream.

A change opens a reason step inside the group that changed, recorded as a category rather than free text, because engineering established that free text could be stored and never read downstream. An empty or unscored tag gets a state of its own, so “the model has nothing” never reads as “the model is certain”, and the quoted evidence sits next to the suggestion on the route to accepting it, so agreeing also means passing the evidence. The screen labels this plainly: AI-identified evidence, and your validation trains the model.

The effect is that a correction arrives categorised, attributable and attached to the evidence it was made against, instead of arriving as nothing at all. That is what makes review auditable rather than decorative.

Designed in support of access control (45 CFR §164.312(a)(1)), audit controls (§164.312(b)), integrity of information (§164.312(c)), and information-access management (§164.308(a)(4)). A reviewer correction inside this workflow is an operational data decision, not a patient’s formal amendment request under §164.526.

I proposed a free-text reason; engineering could not read it downstream.

Part 3 · My work

I designed the moment a reviewer disagrees with the model

I designed the human-review experience around the model’s output, not the model, the pipeline or the release. The prototype at the top of this page is that screen.

Four situations, and what each one costs a reviewer

Correct suggestion: keep the common path light
Nearly every tag is accepted and the queue is measured by the document, so I kept accepting to one action and asked nothing of a reviewer who agrees.
Incorrect suggestion: capture what changed and why
A change opens a reason step inside the group that changed, never at the end of the document, and a short fixed list rather than a text box, because free text could be stored and not read.
Nothing suggested: let the reviewer supply what the model missed
I gave an empty or unscored tag its own state, so “the model has nothing” never looks like “the model is certain”.
Low confidence, show what the model read
A confidence figure was already on screen and had not helped, so I moved the quoted text next to the suggestion, on the route to accepting it, which makes agreeing also mean passing the evidence.

What is mine, and what is not

The accuracy figures above are the system’s, measured before I arrived. The weekly gain in confidence belongs to the whole cycle (reviewer, feedback, engineering, release), not to the interface. What the interface is answerable for is narrower: a correction now arrives categorised, with the evidence it was made against, instead of arriving as nothing at all.

Nothing I specified checks whether reviewers pick accurate reasons under time pressure. I would instrument the reason codes from day one.

I took each tagging proposal back, and every one came back smaller.

I kept a record of how each ticket moved, and why the team sent work back

Three times the team sent it back

I reconstructed these from the decisions they produced, because the original tickets are gone. Each one ended with my first idea getting smaller.

Reconstructed 👨‍💻 Engineering

The reason a reviewer gives for a correction

I proposed

A text field, so a reviewer could say in their own words what was wrong.

Team pushed back

Engineering: free text can be stored, but nothing downstream can read it.

Why

A signal nothing consumes is not a signal.

I changed

A short fixed list, categorised at the moment it is given. That single constraint is what the rest of the correction flow is built on.

Reconstructed 📋 Product · Operations

Where validation gets blocked

I proposed

Block validation at the end of the document until every correction on it had a reason.

Team pushed back

A reviewer could reach the end and be stopped by a group edited several screens earlier.

Why

Possible, and wrong for how the queue is worked. Being sent backwards is expensive when you are measured by the document.

I changed

The reason is answered in the group that changed. Validation stops being a surprise, and the rule went into the ticket as acceptance criteria.

Reconstructed 👨‍💻 Engineering

How confidence actually arrives

I proposed

Print the model’s confidence directly on every suggested tag.

Team pushed back

It arrives as a value, but not for every tag.

Why

The screen assumed the data was more uniform than it was. A blank reads as a confident zero.

I changed

A named level where there is one, and a defined state for a tag the model could not score.

Part 5 · What it fed

Each correction fed a cycle that made the model more confident week by week

  1. Reviewer decision
  2. Structured feedback
  3. Engineering review
  4. Prompt & definition changes
  5. Biweekly update
  6. Repeat

Model confidence moved by roughly 2 percentage points a week while that cycle was running.

Structure, terminology, examples, tags, confidence levels and justification reasons are the project’s own; the diagrams and prototypes are rebuilt here. Internal system and tag names are not reproduced.

Terms used in this project

Billing correspondence
The scanned documents that arrive from payors and move through the pipeline. Every one of them passes through tagging.
Payor
The insurer responsible for a claim.
Denial
A payor's refusal to pay a claim. Most of the correspondence in this queue concerns one.
Tagging
Step five of six in the pipeline, where a reviewer checks the billing tags the model proposed. This is the step I designed.
CARC
Claim Adjustment Reason Code. The standard code saying why a payor adjusted or denied a claim.
RARC
Remittance Advice Remark Code. The supplementary code that adds detail to a CARC.
Validate
The reviewer action that accepts the tags on a document and moves it to the next step.
Confidence
The model's own score on a suggested tag. It does not arrive for every tag, which is why an unscored tag needed a state of its own.
Structured feedback
A correction recorded as a category rather than free text, so engineering can read it when revising prompts, tag definitions and thresholds.
