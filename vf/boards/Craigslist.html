How I Redesigned Used-Car Discovery on Craigslist

I reimagined the iOS experience to help buyers search, compare listings, evaluate seller credibility, and find relevant vehicles with less friction.

Redesigning Craigslist for faster used-car discovery

For a paid product design brief with LiveFront, I redesigned Craigslist’s React Native iOS experience and created an implementation-ready handoff for the used-car discovery flow. View the presentation deck ↗

The brief

LiveFront asked me to rethink Craigslist’s iPhone app. It was a paid design challenge, and I was the designer: I ran the research, made the design decisions, built the Figma file, presented the work in a deck on October 14, and built a coded prototype of the screens.

Craigslist is a two-sided marketplace. Buyers come for local listings, and private sellers and dealers pay to post them. So I started with a simple question: if browsing felt clearer and listings told people more, would buyers trust the app enough to come back? And if they did, would sellers, who pay $5 a listing, have a reason to post again?

I sketched what that could be worth, as an illustration rather than a forecast: every additional $5 post, less an assumed $1 in operating cost, contributes $4 toward recovering the cost of the design work.

Narrowing the problem

Craigslist sells almost everything, and I couldn’t redesign all of it well. I chose one journey: shopping for a used car. That meant two moments in particular, browsing through cars and looking closely at one.

Whatever someone is buying, they’re trying to answer the same few questions before they reach out to a seller: can I afford it, will it fit my space, is it safe to pick up, is it still available, and is it in good condition? A listing that hides those answers makes the whole experience feel awkward, and I wanted the redesign to bring them forward.

Watching people use the current app

I ran usability sessions on the current app, asking people to shop for a car, and I looked closely at every screen they passed through. The friction showed up screen by screen, often as a pause before the next tap:

  • On the first screen, “categories” and “my searches” sit right next to the search field, and taps landed on the wrong one.
  • The cars and trucks category was hard to find inside a long list of every category Craigslist has.
  • Setting a price meant going back through a filter screen that felt confusing.
  • Recent searches repeated the same location on every line, so finding the exact search meant reading each one.
  • On a listing, the trash icon looked like it would delete someone else’s post, and the star didn’t seem to do anything.

Friction like this can make people give up on a search and trust Craigslist a little less.

A note on evidence. The deck I submitted attached participant counts to these findings. When I revised the work in November, I couldn’t trace those numbers back to session notes, tasks or participants, so I’ve left them out here. On each screen below, I say whether a problem came from what people reported or from my own observation.

Deciding how to approach it

Before drawing anything, I set three rules for myself. Touch targets should be comfortable enough to avoid misclicks. Information should have a clear hierarchy that follows what people are trying to do. And the main actions shouldn’t take extra effort for someone with smaller hands, a bigger phone, or both.

I also wanted the redesign to feel modern without feeling foreign. I worked from Apple’s iOS 26 design kit and Human Interface Guidelines, kept text and colour accessibility in mind, and kept Craigslist’s own type and colours, so the layout and components could change while the app still felt like Craigslist. I documented each screen and component against the Apple guideline pages I checked on October 13. Those pages informed my decisions; they don’t prove compliance, and the accessibility items are untested.

A familiar experience, reorganized

I kept the journey people already knew, Home, browsing, search, results and a listing, and reorganized what sat on each screen. The redesigned screens use a different city and sample listings from the screenshots of the current app.

Home: search takes the lead

The search field shared its space with “my searches” (people reported this), the category list filled the first screen (my observation), and the location showed no search radius (people reported that proximity was unclear). I let the search field lead, gave saved searches their own labelled entry, put the full category list behind an “All categories” pill so nearby listings show first, and showed the location, a 25-mile radius and a map of the search area. The tradeoff: categories are no longer visible at a glance, and longtime users may need to relearn the path.

Browsing cars and trucks: show what buyers compare

Category and location were split across two header menus and the radius wasn’t shown (reported), cards showed only a title and price (reported), and filters sat behind an unlabelled icon (my observation). I gathered category, location and radius into one row of pills, put the price on the photo with mileage, seller type, time and area beneath it, and gave Filters and Save search visible labels above the list. Fewer listings fit on one screen as a result.

Search: recent searches you can scan

Every recent search repeated its location, and the only sign of focus was the text cursor (both my observations). Now the query comes first, the scope appears only when it differs, and a focus ring and a scope line show where the search will run.

Results: every filter in one row

An applied price sat in its own row, away from location and category (my observation), and the results didn’t show the search radius (reported). The price now joins the same row as the other filters, with an × to remove it and a count on Filters, and the location and radius pills lead that row. Pills that run past the screen edge need a horizontal scroll.

A listing: clearer facts, a clearer next step

On a real listing I studied, the trash icon could be read as deleting the post, key facts were mixed into the description, and repair needs like “transmission stuck in gear” were buried in prose. I proposed moving the secondary action into a labelled More menu (the menu itself isn’t designed yet), grouped what the seller provided into a details grid with the description and an approximate pickup area, and proposed surfacing condition issues explicitly. For engineering, I proposed using structured seller fields first, extracting only explicit facts from the description, keeping the original text, and marking anything missing as “Not provided.”

These are design responses, and they’re untested. Each one is the benefit I intended, not one I measured.

The final screens

Five key screens in one journey, from Home in Minneapolis to the details of one car.

Try the prototype I built

Screens show how the redesign looks; I wanted people to feel how it behaves. So I built it as a working prototype in HTML, CSS and JavaScript, an iPhone screen at 430 × 932 points, from my handoff and the Figma file.

Try the live prototype ↗ It opens full screen on a phone.

Prototype notes: what’s real, and what’s sample data
  • Sample data. Listings and sellers are fictional. Vehicle photos are AI-generated sample images.
  • Map. Interactive map (Leaflet, OpenStreetMap tiles). Listing positions and search centres are illustrative; distances are straight-line.
  • Not in Figma. Year, make/model, location and distance controls.
  • Unavailable. Favorites, Post, Account, Chat, Saved searches list, Contact seller.
  • Exploratory concept. Save search, alerts, Continue your search and price-drop alerts are a proposed concept, not validated. Notifications are simulated examples; nothing is monitored or delivered.

Discovery journey.

How each screen behaves
  • Home: the map shows the proximity circle only, with no markers.
  • Home sheet position: the sheet starts raised, so the search field, the toolbar and one complete listing row sit above the bottom navigation. The position is measured from the rendered layout, so it adapts to the viewport. It's never lower than the Figma position, and never so high that the map and circle disappear.
  • Home sheet gestures:
    • An upward swipe anywhere in the sheet, including over the search field or a card, expands it. It doesn't open search or focus anything.
    • Once expanded, the listings scroll normally. Pulling down collapses the sheet only when the list is at the top.
    • A deliberate tap on the search field opens search. A drag cancels any card or button activation.
    • The grabber is a button: tap, Enter or Space toggles it, and ArrowUp/ArrowDown expand or collapse it.
    • On desktop, a mouse drag or the wheel does the same.
    • The exposed map still pans and zooms.
  • Search: the search field has recent searches and the "cars" shortcut.
  • Category browsing: each for-sale category works on the sample data.
  • Results: a two-state results sheet. Drag the grabber or header, tap the grabber, or use Expand map / Collapse map.
  • Map view: markers and clusters, a preview for the selected listing, and details.
  • Details: Save star, photo counter, "Not provided" facts, pickup area, and Previous/Next across the filtered results.
  • Returning: closing details restores the screen, view, selection, scroll, filters and the map position.

Filtering: one shared filter state. It drives the list, the result count, the map markers, the previews and listing navigation. It combines:

What the filters combine, and how the panels behave
  • the query
  • location and proximity (straight-line distance from the search centre)
  • category
  • price range
  • seller type
  • in Cars & trucks, vehicle year, mileage, and make and model

How the filter panels behave

  • Drafts: sheets edit a draft. Apply commits it; Cancel, Close, Esc or the scrim discards it. Apply previews the outcome ("Show N results").
  • Filters (n) count: counts price, seller, year, mileage and make/model. "Also filtered: …" lists the active sheet filters.
  • No matches: the query and pills stay visible, with an explanation and the recovery actions that apply.
  • One panel at a time: each sits on the bottom edge with safe-area spacing, scrolls when it's long, and keeps its footer above the keyboard.
  • Focus: focus moves into the panel, Tab stays inside it, and focus returns to the control that opened it.

Returning to a car search (exploratory concept, not validated). Built from the Figma section "Exploration · Returning to a car search" (R0–R11), and labelled as a concept in the desktop notes.

What the concept does
  • Save search: saves the query, category, location, radius and every applied filter. New-match alerts are optional (Off, Daily, Weekly); declining still saves. A "Search saved" confirmation offers Manage.
  • Continue your search: a Home entry for the most recent saved search, with "n new" since it was last viewed. Selecting it restores the search; viewing resets the count.
  • New-match alert: an example notification opens the saved results, with "1 new since …" and the new card marked "New".
  • Price-drop alert: saving a listing (star) offers price-drop alerts. The example notification opens the same listing at its new price, inside its saved search. The card then shows "Price lowered from $8,900".
  • Manage: change the frequency, pause alerts (Off), or remove the saved search.
  • Removed listing: shows "This listing was removed by the seller" with "See matching results".
  • Storage: saved searches, price-drop alerts and demo events persist in localStorage, separate from saved listings.
  • Notifications are in-app examples only. Nothing monitors in the background, nothing is pushed or emailed, and no notification permission is requested.

What I’d do next

The redesigned screens and the prototype haven’t been tested with car shoppers yet, so the next step is to put this prototype in front of the people it was designed for and see which of these decisions actually reduce the hesitation I saw.

Revising this project also changed how I present research. I’d rather show fewer findings I can trace to their source than numbers I can’t stand behind.
