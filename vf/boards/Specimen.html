How I Redesigned Oncology Specimen Kits for Safer Handling

I redesigned a tissue collection kit so the preparation instructions sit in the lid and the specimen has its own cavity to sit in.

Role
Support UX designer: instruction lid redesign, in-box storage proposal
Team
Two senior designers and me, working as a subteam
Phase
Two-week engagement; lid redesign and a documented foam insert

≈$75K–$90K

$ value per 10K kits shipped

How this is calculated

What it represents The two figures above, taken across a production run rather than a single kit.

Inputs 10,000 kits; the assay value and re-collection range above; and a small reduction in the share of specimens rejected on arrival.

The logic Value here is rejection rate × volume × cost per rejection. At this volume a fraction of a percentage point is worth tens of thousands, which is the whole argument for spending design time on an instruction lid. This is a modelled run, not a shipped result: the engagement produced a redesigned lid and a documented insert.

≈$1K–$30K

$ re-collection cost avoided

How this is calculated

What it represents The cost of going back to the patient for another specimen after a rejection.

Why it is a range It depends entirely on what has to be repeated. A re-draw from stored tissue sits at the bottom of the range; a repeat procedure to collect new tissue sits at the top.

The logic The kit cannot change the cost of a re-collection. It can only change how often one is triggered. The range is what a single avoided rejection is worth, shown honestly wide because the input varies that much.

≈$3.5K

$ assay value protected per kit

How this is calculated

What it represents The value riding on one correctly prepared specimen; what is lost if the lab rejects it.

Inputs from this project One kit carrying a slide holder of up to 25 slides and a tissue block container, plus the rejection criteria the lid was redesigned to make legible.

The logic A rejected specimen writes off the whole assay, not part of it. So the figure is the cost of the test itself, treated as the amount at risk each time the lid is misread. It is what a mistake costs, not a saving claimed.

I audited the instruction lid against the pack-out decisions it asked for.

I worked from the kit, the sheet, and the onsite research I was synthesising. Below I keep what I observed separate from what I concluded.

Observed

  • The slide count is the only value that changes between the three rows.
  • The base of the box has no insert, tray or liner.
  • The specimen travels as a rigid slide holder or as a tissue block container.

What I took it to mean

  • Repeating the constant and burying the variable makes the reader compare rows to find the one number that differs.
  • Criteria at the foot of the page are read after the decision they were meant to prevent.
  • An empty base leaves the clinician to decide where the specimen sits.

I had no interview transcripts or usage figures, so nothing here is measured.

I found the slide count was the only value changing down the table.

A clinician prepares tissue to a specification, packs it out and ships it to the lab. Opening the kit, I found that specification on a single sheet in the lid, and the base below it empty.

The five findings

  1. Hard to scan. Compressed type and tight leading, which makes it slow and error-prone.
  2. Repeated information. The block specification is printed three times, and the detail that differentiates the tests (the slide count) gets lost inside it.
  3. Critical warnings buried. The rejection criteria are the costliest thing to get wrong and the least visible thing on the sheet.
  4. Specimen movement. Nothing secures the block or the slide container in transit.

Nobody was re-tooling the box, so I left the sheet where it was and changed the order of what it said.

The slide count was the only value that changed down the table

Same requirement for every test, so I collapsed it into one cell spanning three rows, which leaves the slide count as the only thing that changes.

Criteria at the foot of the page are read after the decision

They were a run-in list under the table. I pulled them into a labelled row of six, each named under its own mark.

I reordered the instruction lid rather than re-tool a box already in manufacture.

Patient rights & protections

I designed the kit around the fact that every specimen belonged to a patient, not a shipping process.

A specimen is finite. Tumour tissue cannot be re-collected casually, and a rejection on arrival writes off the whole assay, so the requirements that decide acceptance are the highest-stakes content in the box. I treated three responsibilities as separate: whether the specimen survives preparation, whether the clinician can follow the handling instructions, and whether the container is physically protected in transit.

A printed lid is an exposure surface like any screen, so what it could carry was reviewed before the redesign moved into iteration and again as the layout changed. That check is why the sheet stayed preparation guidance: requirements and rejection criteria, with patient information kept off it.

The rejection criteria had been a run-in list at the foot of the sheet, read after the decision they were meant to prevent. I pulled them into a labelled row of six, each named under its own mark, and collapsed the constant requirement into one cell so the slide count is the only value that changes down the table. The lid is preparation guidance and nothing else: it carries what a clinician must do to the specimen, not information about the patient.

The empty base was the other half. A container travelling loose is a container that can arrive compromised, so I specified a one-piece die-cut insert with a cavity shaped to each carrier, which gives the specimen one place to sit rather than leaving the clinician to decide.

Designed in support of specimen identification, handling and rejection criteria under CLIA (42 CFR §493.1242), and handling of potentially infectious material under OSHA (29 CFR §1910.1030), with patient information kept off the instruction surface in line with the minimum necessary standard (45 CFR §164.502(b)). The project documentation I worked from did not establish a shipping classification, so no transport standard is claimed here.

The foam insert I specified

I specified one-piece die-cut polyethylene, with a large cavity for a slide holder of up to 25 slides and a small cavity for a tissue block container, sized for a standard 8 × 8 × 2.5 in mailer.

The four kit configurations it holds

I found the tissue block container travelling loose in an empty kit base.

The instructions print on panel A, the inner lid. The foam sits on panel B, the base.

I fitted both changes onto Panel A and Panel B of the existing box.

The subteam was three people meeting three times a week for two weeks. One senior designer owned direction and one contributed design. I was the support UX designer.

I wrote the subteam’s tickets and kept their statuses current.

Neither change added information. I moved a block specification across three rows, raised the criteria above the fold, and cut a cavity to the shape of the container so there is one place to put it.

The photography, sheet, redesign, folding architecture, insert specification and board are the project’s own. The company name and mark are blurred in the artwork.

I learned neither change added information; both were about placement.

Terms used in this project

Specimen kit
The box a clinician uses to prepare and ship tissue to the lab.
Instruction lid
The printed sheet inside the lid. It carried the whole preparation specification, including the rejection criteria.
Tissue block
A preserved tissue sample in its container. It is one of the two things the kit ships.
Slide holder
A rigid carrier for up to 25 prepared slides. It is the other thing the kit ships.
Pack-out
Preparing and packing the specimen for shipment, the step where the lid is read.
Rejection criteria
The conditions under which the lab refuses a specimen on arrival. This was the costliest thing to get wrong and sat lowest on the sheet.
Assay
The test run on the specimen once it reaches the lab. A rejection writes off its whole value, not part of it.
Re-collection
Going back to the patient for another specimen after a rejection.
Die-cut foam insert
The one-piece polyethylene insert I specified, cut with a cavity shaped to each container.
Panel A and Panel B
The inner lid and the base of the folded box. The instructions print on A and the foam sits on B.
